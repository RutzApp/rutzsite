Missed a Run? Here's What to Do

If you missed a run, don't make it up — skip it and carry on with the plan. One missed run changes nothing. A missed week needs one easier week to return. Two weeks or more means stepping back and rebuilding for a few weeks. Here's the full playbook.

Get a plan that adapts

You missed one run

Skip it and move on. Fitness is built over months; a single session is a rounding error. The mistake is not missing the run — it's trying to squeeze it in tomorrow, on top of tomorrow's run.

The one exception: if the run you missed was the week's long run and you have a free day within the next two, move it. Then drop or shorten the easy run that was there, so the week's total doesn't grow.

You missed a whole week

Repeat the week before the one you missed, at slightly lower volume, then continue. You will have lost almost no fitness: it takes roughly two weeks of no running before aerobic fitness meaningfully drops.

Do not jump back in at the week the calendar says you should be on. That is a bigger week than the last one you completed, after a week of nothing — exactly the load spike that causes injuries.

You missed two weeks or more

Step back and rebuild. Start at around 50-60% of your previous weekly volume with easy running only, and add roughly 10-15% a week until you're back. Reintroduce hard sessions only once the volume feels normal again.

Then look honestly at the race date. With 8 or more weeks left, you can usually still run well with an adjusted goal. With less, consider changing the target from a time to a strong finish — or moving to a later race.

You're travelling

Plan for less and you'll do more. Treat a travel week as a recovery week: short easy runs, no long run, no pressure. Thirty minutes around a new city counts.

  • Run in the morning before the day takes over.
  • Swap intensity for consistency — three short easy runs beat one heroic session.
  • Crossing time zones? Keep everything easy for the first two or three days.
  • Schedule the big week before or after the trip, never during it.

You've been ill

Use the neck rule. Symptoms above the neck only — a blocked nose, a mild sore throat — and easy running is generally fine if you feel up to it. Anything below the neck — chest congestion, fever, body aches, stomach trouble — means no running until it has cleared.

Return with a few days of short, easy runs before anything hard. After a fever or a chest infection, take it slower still, and see a doctor if you're unsure. This is general guidance, not medical advice.

What not to do

Almost every comeback injury traces to one of these.

  • Doubling up: two runs' worth of distance in one day to catch up.
  • Stacking hard days back to back because the quality sessions slipped.
  • Resuming at the calendar week instead of the last week you actually completed.
  • Skipping the taper to fit in missed long runs.
  • Quitting the plan because it's no longer perfect. No one's ever is.

Missed training: common questions

Should I make up a missed run?

No. Skip it and continue with the plan. Making up runs bunches your training together and raises injury risk far more than the missed session costs you in fitness.

How much fitness do I lose in a week off running?

Very little. Aerobic fitness holds for roughly 10 to 14 days without running. After a week off you may feel rusty for a run or two, but the fitness is still there.

I missed my long run. Should I do it midweek?

Only if it fits within the next day or two and you shorten or drop another run so the week's total doesn't grow. Otherwise let it go and do next week's long run as planned.

Can I still run my marathon after missing two weeks of training?

Usually yes, if there are 8 or more weeks left. Rebuild gradually over two to three weeks and adjust your goal time. If the gap fell in the final six weeks, aim to finish strong rather than chase a personal best.

How do I keep training while travelling for work?

Treat it as a lighter week: short easy runs, preferably in the morning, and no long run. Put your bigger training weeks either side of the trip.
